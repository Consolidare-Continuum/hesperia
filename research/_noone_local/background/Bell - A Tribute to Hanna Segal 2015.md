                                               Introduction
Hanna Segal’s contribution to psychoanalysis was marked not only by its
depth but by its extraordinary range. More than anyone else working within
the Kleinian framework, she has demonstrated the relevance of psychoana-
lytic ideas to human knowledge in general; her papers on aesthetics, symbol-
ism, literature and socio-political issues have made fundamental and original
contributions which have influenced intellectual life far afield from psycho-
analysis per se. It might, thus, seem reasonable to make a division in Segal’s
work between the clinico-theoretical contributions and what one may think of
as the ‘applied work’. This however should be resisted, for this latter aspect of
her work has not arisen from her ‘setting about’ applying psychoanalytic
knowledge to other objects, but has always been emergent from her immedi-
ate clinical and theoretical concerns. Her classic paper on aesthetics (Segal,
1952) can be read both as a clinical discussion of patients who have difficulties
in their creative work and, at one and the same time, as a contribution to the
central questions of aesthetics. Her study of Joseph Conrad (Segal, 1984)
though on one level a psychoanalytic exegesis of his work, turns out to have
immediate clinical psychoanalytic relevance as an examination of the roots of
creativity in the depressive position and the relation of this to the mid-life cri-
sis. Her papers on the nuclear mentality and the triumphalism of the West
emerge directly from her explorations of the nature and phenomenology of
the death drive when fuelled by manic omnipotence, the denial that is central
to its nature and the destruction it brings in its wake. So, her work cannot be
divided in this way for it evinces a compelling unity.
   This unity of thinking is I believe central to the kind of thing psychoanal-
ysis is; Janus faced, it looks at one and the same time inwards towards the

characteristic of psychoanalysis that is rooted in its history. Although there
are of course works, such as Totem and Taboo (1912), The Future of an Illu-
sion (1927), Civilisation and its Discontents (1930), where Freud addresses
human culture specifically, it would be an important misunderstanding to
consider these works as his principal contribution to this area of knowledge.
For the cultural relevance of psychoanalysis runs like a thread through all
his work. This broad reach of intellectual engagement, so characteristic of
psychoanalysis, finds its antagonist in the ever-present pressure to divide up
the intellectual map, creating separate domains and territorial boundaries.
Here, intellectual engagement degrades into a culture of experts, the search
for knowledge and understanding is substituted by technologies of control.
   This division of intellectual labour is naturally, and necessarily, resisted
by psychoanalysis, and where it is not (for example where the reach of psy-
choanalysis is reduced only to a form of psychological treatment) what is
left is not psychoanalysis but a degraded parody of what it was. I am here
reminded of a remark made by that great Marxist cultural critic Raymond
Williams who, when asked at a party “What is your discipline”, retorted:
“Mine is of the pre-enclosure kind” (New Welsh Review, 2014, pp 8–16) –
a comment that beautifully condenses two major trajectories of his thought:
a protest against the division of the disciplines and, through the artful use
of the word ‘enclosure’, making manifest the historical connection between
this division of labour and that pivotal moment in the history of capitalism,
the enclosure of land.
   This way of conceptualising psychoanalysis has epistemological implica-
tions – it foregrounds psychoanalysis as a body of knowledge of mind and
human culture. I have discussed this at greater length in Bell D (1998). As
such, it may be expected to make important contributions to any area of
enquiry whose terrain involves the human subject – such as psychology,
sociology, literature, philosophy of mind, socio-political theory, cultural the-
ory . . . and many more. This is not to suggest, and this is a common misun-
derstanding, that explanation of the social and cultural can be reduced to
individual psychology, but merely that an appreciation of the inner life of
the human subject will by necessity make an important contribution to
understanding.1 For example, economic, social, historical and political
explorations of the phenomenon of Nazism will be enriched by an under-
standing of the attractions (and this will include phenomena well outside
consciousness) of this perverse ideology. However, given that what psycho-
analysis has to say may not be entirely welcomed, a certain steadfastness is
required if it to maintain its place at the table.
   I have emphasised this dimension of psychoanalysis, as Hanna Segal was
one of its finest exponents. Segal writes in the preface to her first book that
her discipline is psychoanalysis and by that she clearly meant the non-
enclosed version!

                     Segal’s contribution to psychoanalysis
My discussion of Segal’s work will fall into three sections. A review of some
of her main theoretical contributions will be followed by a more informal
account of her as a person. In the last section I will explore some of her
socio-political contributions.
   In considering her theoretical contribution it is necessary to consider the
historical context. The years between 1941 and 1945, during which Hanna
Segal entered into the psychoanalytic world, were those in which the British
Psycho-Analytical Society was immersed in the political turmoil and debate
of the Controversial Discussions, which centred on Melanie Klein’s discov-
eries (King and Steiner, 1991). It should be remembered that Freud had
died only two years earlier and to some extent opposing sides were preoccu-
pied with the issue of what counted as a genuine development of his work
and what represented a major deviation.
   This period not only generated a lot of heat but also shed considerable
light, as partisans of differing views were impelled to clarify their positions.
This resulted in the presentation of papers that have remained seminal to the
development of psychoanalysis in Britain. This was especially so with Susan
Isaacs’ paper ‘The nature and function of phantasy’, (Isaacs, 1948). The
issues raised in these discussions are not only of historical interest, for they
frequently resurface refracted through the lens of seemingly contemporary
issues - such as in discussions of the therapeutic alliance, the ‘real relation-
ship’, and the accessibility of deeply unconscious primitive phantasy. These
points of controversy go to the heart of our understanding of the psychoana-
lytic conception of the mind and the nature of the psychoanalytic task.
   In 1946, the year after Segal’s qualification, Klein published her ground-
breaking paper, ‘Notes on schizoid mechanisms’ (Klein, 1946), where she
described very primitive mental mechanisms that underlie serious psycho-
pathological states. It was in this paper that she described clearly the split-
ting of the ego and the projection of parts of the self into objects and
clarified the anxieties and motives that underlie these processes.
   Klein’s discoveries in this area provided the basis for her analysands,
Segal, Rosenfeld and subsequently Bion, to carry this work forward
through their treatment of psychotic patients. Rosenfeld, in 1947, described
a borderline patient who developed a transference psychosis that he dealt
with purely interpretatively, and in the same year Segal presented her mem-
bership paper, ‘Some aspects of the analysis of a schizophrenic’, which was
the first report in the literature of a patient schizophrenic at the time of
referral and treated without any fundamental alterations in classical psycho-
analytic technique.
   One of the hallmarks of the Kleinian tradition is to be found in the rela-
tion between theory and practice. Theoretical formulations have a quality
of being ‘experience near’ and some have seen this as a weakness of the the-
ory, even a reification, believing it to make insufficient distinction between
clinical descriptions of events, and models of mental function which are
held to operate at a higher level of abstraction. For Klein and her followers
this distinction does not hold and some would argue that it would not hold

description was implicit from the start, but was given its most elegant theo-

   Isaacs took the view, and this view has remained central to the present
day, that the primary content of the unconscious is unconscious phantasy.
Isaacs showed how those processes we call mental mechanisms – projection,
introjection, identification etc. – are descriptions, so to speak, from the out-
side. From the inside, that is from the perspective of the internal world of
the subject, they are lived through the medium of unconscious phantasy.
Thus, projection is associated with unconscious phantasies of the subject
ejecting aspects of the self into an object; introjection is associated with tak-
ing into the self aspects of the object which can then become the basis of
identification.
   These phantasies all ultimately depend upon the experience of bodily pro-
cesses, as it is the mind’s perception of these bodily processes that forms the
building blocks of all later psychological experience, as Freud put it ‘the
ego is first and foremost a bodily ego’ (1923). Wollheim (1969) has dis-
cussed the implications of this from a philosophical perspective. Phantasies
are the way, according to his account, that the mind represents to itself its
own activities. But, these phantasies are not just representations, akin to a
film going in parallel with the mental mechanisms, it is more a unitary phe-
nomenon. For the way the mind phantasises its own activities is consequen-
tial for the structure and functioning of the mind. The phantasy of
projection into objects of vital parts of the self, good or bad, may result in
a feeling of emptiness but this is more than a feeling, it is the mind’s accu-
rate perception of its own state consequent on projection, that is, it really is
depleted.
   My reason for focusing on this is not only that it is central to the Klei-
nian way of thinking about theory and clinical experience, but because it
was so central to Hanna Segal’s work. Her papers show a remarkable
facility for using clinical examples that vividly show the theory in action.
This was used to great effect in her first book, ‘An Introduction to the
Work of Melanie Klein’ (1964), which has become a standard text. In this
work she elucidates Klein’s ideas demonstrating how they work in action,
all the time bringing clinical examples of her own to illustrate theoretical
points. As Segal put it to me, ‘My main interest is unconscious phantasy,
the way it expresses itself, its relation to reality, internal and external, and
its relation to perception’. Her extraordinary facility with dream material,
which occupies a central position in all her published papers, bears testa-
ment to this.

‘A psychoanalytic approach to aesthetics’ although not published until
1952 was in fact the first paper that Segal read to the British Psycho-Ana-
lytical Society in 1947, two years after she qualified. In this paper she
brings together her psychoanalytic preoccupations and her involvement in
art and literature. This paper was stimulated by her having in analysis

creative work. Both these groups of patients brought to the analysis fun-

the analysis a link between the paralysis in their work and their inability
to mourn. This led Segal to explore the roots of creativity in the depres-
sive position, and to develop a theory of aesthetics that for the first time
provided a basis for understanding, from a psychological point of view,
what it is about great art that makes it universal and enduring. Freud’s
work in this area largely centred on two approaches: the discovery, in the
content of the work of art, of universal human themes, and an exploration
of those infantile situations, from the artist’s own history, that are
reflected in the work. An analysis based only on content would find simi-
lar themes in the great Greek tragedies and in evanescent pulp dramas
and so fails to distinguish between them. As Segal puts it, referring to ear-
lier papers by Freud and others, ‘They dealt with points of psychological
interest but not with the central question of aesthetics, which is: what con-
stitutes good art and in what essential respects is it different from other
human works, from bad art in particular’. Further, such an approach
could never answer the question as to how the artist achieves his effect
upon his audience
   Freud lacked adequate conceptual equipment for addressing these prob-
lems, though as is so often the case in Freud, this did not prevent him fol-
lowing his own intuition via a more literary, anecdotal route. In ‘On
Transience’ (Freud, 1916), written at approximately the same time as he
wrote ‘Mourning and Melancholia’, he discusses a poet (now known to be
Rilke) and a ‘taciturn’ friend who could not enjoy the beauty of their
mountain walk because it brought to awareness the transience of all life.
This was not a problem for Freud. As he put it, ‘Transience value is scar-
city value in time. . . a flower that blossoms only for a single night does
not seem to us on that account less lovely.’ (Freud 1916 p.305). He agreed
that awareness of all beauty must bring thoughts of death and the passing
of all things but, as he pointed out, ‘. . . since the value of all this beauty
and perfection is determined by its significance for our own emotional
lives, it has no need to survive us and is therefore independent of absolute
duration’ (p 306). These considerations, however, had no effect on his
friends which led him to the conclusion that a ‘powerful emotional factor
was at work’ and he went on to describe this as ‘a revolt in their minds
against mourning’(p306).
   Segal puts the capacity to mourn, now enriched by Klein’s elucidation
of the inner struggle that forms the basis of the depressive position, both
at the centre of the artist’s work and of the audience’s aesthetic response.
Works of art derive their aesthetic depth from the artist’s capacity to face
the pain and guilt inherent in his perception of damage done to his good
objects and, through his creation of the work, to give substance to this
struggle, the work itself being an act of reparation. We the audience are
gripped by such works as we identify with the author’s confrontation with
the pain of his shattered internal world and obtain reassurance from his
ability, through intense psychic work, to overcome it and depict it in his

made his observing of his need to write, and the functions it served for
him, the basis of his work. He linked the creative work with the work of
mourning, describing a book as a ‘vast graveyard where on most of the
tombstones one can read no more the faded names’.3 Segal describes how
the artist, like the neurotic, suffers from all the pain and terror of a
destroyed inner world but, unlike the neurotic, does not depend upon
magical solutions.
   The artist has a highly developed awareness of the pain of his inner world
but also the capacity to endure it and, through his equally highly developed
reality sense4, he gives form to his struggle, creating through it a real object,
always a communication to his fellow human beings.
   Segal’s patients could not bear the pain of these inner situations; like
Freud’s companions, their minds revolted against mourning and so were
blocked not only in their artistic endeavours but also in other creative
aspects of life, particularly in their sexuality. The incapacity to mourn
appeared to arise from two principal sources: inability to bear the pain of
guilt on the one hand, and on the other, intolerance of the separateness
from the object that mourning inevitably brings, in other words for narcis-
sistic reasons.
   Segal reflects further both on the relation of form to content and on the
nature of beauty, our love and terror of it. She points out that if beauty is
the basis of one aspect of aesthetic experience then its contrary cannot be
ugliness but only ‘aesthetically indifferent’. Ugliness, being part of human
experience, can evoke aesthetic reactions that are in many ways similar to
our response to beauty; Segal provides, through her model, a solution to
this apparent paradox. Both beauty and ugliness can together provide the
basis for profound aesthetic experience; in the great Greek tragedies the
ugliness is in the content whilst the beauty is in the form.

him that I understood what he wanted from me, why he wanted it so des-

splitting that lies at the root of the illness. Also, the maintenance of an idea-

with, from the patient’s point of view, the analyst’s tacit support, and most
likely towards the members of his own family who would be ill equipped to
deal with it. For her patient tried desperately to recruit his analyst as an
ally against his various persecutors which included his doctors and various
members of his family.

that is, not strengthening the defence mechanisms of the patient but bring-

the initial stages putting great pressure on his analyst to break boundaries,
proved himself to be capable of using interpretations, could be relieved by
the understanding of his impulses and was not overwhelmed by the bringing

consisted of two basic phenomena, which were connected (Bion, 1962a, was
later to describe this as ‘the selected fact’).

also referred to counting the number of people in his family, counting the

   These two papers taken together bring concerns which were to remain cen-
tral to Segal’s contributions to both theory and technique in psychoanalysis.
   The paper on the treatment of a schizophrenic and various others that fol-

junction with the work of Bion, Rosenfeld and Henri Rey, became central to
this development of psychoanalysis. However, she has never liked to consider
herself a specialist on psychosis, being rather wary of ‘specialisms’ of this
sort, feeling they can lead to an over-dramatisation, even romanticisation of
the work. She said ‘I only have one specialism and that is psychoanalysis’9.
   Segal has suggested that although, theoretically, one might imagine that
such patients are the most difficult to treat and the most demanding for the
analyst, she has not found this always to be the case in practice, borderline
patients being often even more demanding. There are probably a variety of
reasons for this. She has made the interesting suggestion that central to the
difficulty in borderline cases is that there is always a catastrophe about to
happen, and this hangs over the analysis as a constant threat. The patient
may break down and the analyst is bound to feel he is to blame and, as she
has pointed out, this is in a certain sense true. With a schizophrenic patient,
though confronted with difficulties of a different nature, the worst has
already happened which to some extent allows the analyst more freedom to
operate.

although the phantasy is depressive in content (fears for the survival of his

   First, insight is therapeutic because it leads to the regaining and integra-

   Something akin to this nightmare scenario of annihilation is part of the
inner world of some psychotic patients but, as Segal makes clear, all of us
are prone to such anxieties to some degree. Thus threat of nuclear war con-
fronts us with our worst unconscious terrors made real, and so recognition
of its true significance is particularly unbearable; this underlies the tendency
to be reassured by the crazy pronouncement of ‘limited nuclear strikes’. In
Britain the government even issued guidebooks on how to survive a nuclear
attack. As Segal points out, they were, not surprisingly, uninterested in the
response of The British Medical Association to their civil defence plans.
The BMA pointed out that given that there could be no preparation for a
nuclear blast and that after such a blast there would be no communications,
no doctors, no nurses and no food, making any preparation for survival
was entirely meaningless.
   Segal’s paper is a passionate ‘cri de coeur’ and she took her title from a
passage in the book ‘Hope Against Hope’ by Nadezhda Mandelstam
(1971). Her husband, the poet Osip Mandelstam, was hounded by the Sta-
linists and eventually sent to the Gulag. Nadezhda Mandelstam contem-
plates what sort of reaction might be the appropriate human response when
facing such horrors and warns of the dangers of the stoic quiet acceptance
that some might regard as dignified. She thinks of the cow on its way to
the slaughter, of its bellows, kicks and screeches, seeing this as the more
appropriate and more human response - maybe, just maybe, somebody will
hear. As she puts it, ‘silence is the real crime’.
   With the collapse of the Eastern Bloc many were lulled into thinking that
the world was now a safer place. Segal was wary of being drawn into any
false sense of security. Soon the initial hopes brought by ‘Perestroika’ gave
way to an escalation of poverty, inequality and all the related social prob-
lems. In ‘Hiroshima, the Gulf war and after’ (1995), she focuses attention
on a particular new danger the world faces. Drawing on insights from her
clinical work, she describes how, when paranoia lessens, there can be a real
possibility of mental development. This is, however, inevitably associated
with a great deal of pain, as the individual comes to recognise as delusional
the world he has occupied, the damage that this has caused. However, if
this pain cannot be stood there can be a dangerous manic response where
the self is idealised, all damage denied, bringing further destruction in its
wake, and so re-creating the paranoid world.
   Drawing on the work of Bion, Segal shows how these extreme defensive
manoeuvres are intensified in groups, most especially those large groups
that we call ‘nations’. The collapse of the cold war did not lead to any res-
titutive efforts and armaments were maintained. The inability to face press-
ing problems ‘at home’, such as poverty, unemployment and recognition of
the terrible waste of human resources brought about by the arms race, cre-

   The western ideologues, triumphant over the collapse of the Soviet Union
and the eastern bloc, now believed they could rule the world.18 Any

tempt for the United Nations. Perry Anderson captures well the
transformation resulting from the falling away of ideological opposition: we
face a world where ‘without any victory, the adversary has gone’ . . . a
world of no alternative.
   Segal brings here, to great effect, her deep understanding of the nature
and vicissitudes of omnipotence and in doing so develops her concept of
‘manic triumphalism’ - a concept that is at once both a clinical and social.
Manic triumphalism has a hatred of any obstacle, violent sweeping away
any obstruction in its path. Given that such an obstruction is created by the
capacity for thought, thinking itself is treated as an enemy and regarded
with paranoid sensitivity; see for example George Bush’s paranoid state-
ment: “Every nation, in every region, now has a decision to make. Either
you are with us, or you are with the terrorists”.
   In the face of these situations, Segal believes that psychoanalysts should
not be neutral as some contend. Analytic neutrality for her refers to the
neutrality of the analyst in the session, but when it comes to the world
around us, Segal believed that the analyst had to also embrace her role as
citizen. And further, because analysts have a particular understanding of
the creative and destructive processes in the individual mind, processes
which are intensified in social movements, they have a responsibility to
counter the deceptions of the perverse ideologies that our rulers thrust upon
us, and also to struggle to understand the nature of their appeal. As she has
put it, believing in the importance of analytic neutrality in the clinical set-
ting should not be confused with neutering oneself when it comes to think-
ing about the nature of the world that we live in (personal communication).
   Segal’s intervention in these situations, in the tradition of critical theory,
unmasks the subjectivities that are created by and are required for, the
maintenance of the grip of these ideologies; subjectivities that obscure the
vested interest that they serve. And here Segal uses, to great effect, her
remarkable capacity to capture the content and dynamics of the uncon-

                                The degradation of politics
Segal used Bion’s model of group functioning to make an important contri-
bution to understanding the degradation of politics in our contemporary
world. She pointed out that Bion’s (1961) differentiation between the ‘work
group’ and the ‘basic assumption group’ is lacking in the case in politics, at
least in politics as it is now lived. In a sense there is no work group, as the
work of the politician is to create and foster basic assumption mentality,
that is to attract support not through argument but through the promotion
of regression,’ Follow me, I will transform the economy, make everyone
safe, create law and order’ and coming not far behind, ‘get rid of those par-
asites, the immigrants from abroad and shirkers at home, who leach on the
civilised values of England’s honest decent working people’. The promotion
of regressive projective systems becomes the key to political success.
   But this ideology with all its absurd propagandistic proclamations has its
attractions - particularly for those who are the most insecure, who feel their
place in the world is under threat, are burdened with a sense of their own
superfluousness,19 actual or threatened. This intolerable situation is dealt
with in the time honoured way: the hated uncertainty/sense of superfluous-
ness is projected into others - it is not us who have no place, who are super-
fluous, it is them (immigrants, social security scroungers etc.). This does
bring some relief. But because the ‘belonging’ it provides is based not on
understanding but on projection, because the community created is not a
real community but a pseudo-community founded not on real shared ties
but only on negation – we are alike because we are not them – this whole
situation is precarious. But the sane awareness of the deception and lies that
are at the foundation of these delusions is never completely eradicated, and
so the drive for projection is ever more desperate. Adorno’s acute percep-
tion of the nature of the fascist mob is noteworthy in this context. Even
they, he avers, know at some level that the whole structure of their belief
system is based on lies. He writes:

      It is probably the suspicion of this fictitiousness of their own group psychology that
      makes fascist crowds so merciless and unapproachable. If they would stop to rea-
      son for a second, the whole performance would fall to pieces and they would be left
      in panic                                                       (Adorno T, 1991, p. 152)

   We are certainly living in difficult times. In 2006 Segal wrote: “What does
the future hold? It is pretty grim because global oppression, which includes
mass murder as well as total economic exploitation, leaves desperate terror-
ism as almost the only weapon for the oppressed. . .This expanding global

capacity for balance as his main theme. He had in mind her consideration
of the life-promoting and destructive forces in the human mind. In the
course of this tribute, I have had cause to mention various other polarities
which she approached in the same manner – such as between theory and
technique, internal and external, phantasy and reality, environment and
constitution. Her heritage provided her with a deep love of all things human
but most especially those two great pillars of human achievement which I
believe she so well embodied, Reason and Passion.
