## 01 Mindset First (before numbers, why)
Your market doesn’t care about your idea. It cares about its own problem.
### Think about this
Are you doing market research to:
- convince investors, or
- make better decisions?
Most founders do it for the deck. The ones who win do it for themselves.
Everything in this section is about shifting that default.
### Research reality check
- If your research doesn’t change decisions, it’s not research.
- Your market size is only useful if it guides the next step you take.

### The practical rule
Aim to replace “I think” with “I can explain how we know.”

## 03 Market Sizing (TAM, SAM, SOM — done right)
A number without a method is just a guess with a dollar sign.
### Mindset: TAM ≠ your execution plan
Investors see TAM/SAM/SOM slides all the time.
Your job is to make the numbers believable with assumptions and logic.
---
### Definitions (what each one actually means)
#### TAM — Total Addressable Market
Every possible customer globally if you had zero constraints.
**The universe of opportunity.**
Example: Global project management software: **$15B**
#### SAM — Serviceable Addressable Market
The portion you can realistically reach with your current product, model, and geography.
**Your realistic reach.**
Example: SMBs in Eastern Europe using SaaS: **$180M**
#### SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market
What you can realistically WIN in year 1–3, given competition and your GTM capacity.
**Your year 1 target.**
Example: Moldova + Romania SMBs, year 1–3: **$4M**
### The most important number
It’s not TAM. It’s **SOM** — and can you defend it.
---
## The 1% Fallacy (the trap most founders fall into)
“The global EdTech market is $400B. We just need 1%.”
1% of $400B = **$4B** — that’s bigger than most Series C companies.  
You have no GTM to get there.
Also:
- The market doesn’t know you exist.
- You can’t “capture” a market. You earn customers one by one.
- Investors hear “1% of TAM” and discount your credibility.
### The fix
Size from the bottom up.
Start with customers you can name.
---
## The Approach (a practical 4-step framework)
### 1) Define the problem, not the product
Who has the pain?
How often? How bad?
What do they do today?
Research the customer first — product second.
### 2) Segment the market into layers
Start broad (industry), go narrow (geography, company size, buyer role).
Your beachhead is the smallest segment you can dominate.
### 3) Find real data (primary + secondary)
Secondary: Statista, IBISWorld, Crunchbase, etc.  
Primary: 10–20 customer conversations.
One without the other is weak.
### 4) Validate assumptions — don’t “prove yourself right”
Research isn’t confirmation bias.
If data contradicts your idea, that’s the most valuable finding you’ll get.

## Research Toolkit
### Secondary research (free / fast starting points)
- Statista: market size, growth rates, industry breakdowns
- EU Open Data Portal: government stats for European markets
- Google Trends: whether demand is growing or shrinking (search intent)
- LinkedIn Search: estimate SAM by job title + industry + region, then count
- Crunchbase: who’s funded, valuations, competitor counts
- G2 / Capterra: competitor reviews = ICP language and unmet needs (gold mine)
- CB Insights: market maps, VC investment trends, sector measurement
- SimilarWeb (free): competitor traffic estimates (validate whether demand is real)
---
### Primary research (highest signal)
The most underrated source: talking to people.
- Customer interviews: 10–20 conversations
  Ask: current process, cost, frequency, what they tried, what hurts most.
- Reddit / Facebook Groups: see what people complain about and what tools they hate
- LinkedIn cold outreach: message 50 people in your ICP; aim for 20 minutes
- App store reviews of competitors: 1-star reviews often explain what’s broken
- Competitor job listings: hiring (e.g., 10 sales engineers) signals growth focus

## Methodology (two sizing methods)
### Top-Down (use cautiously)
“Global market is $5B. We’ll capture 1%.”
Pros:
- fast
- easy to produce
Cons:
- often disconnected from reality
- investors see right through it
- no path from number to execution
### Bottom-Up (recommended)
“# customers × price = real revenue potential”
Pros:
- forces you to define your ICP clearly
- matches GTM reality
- investors trust it significantly more
- every assumption is defensible
Example:
5,000 SMBs in Moldova × €600/year = €3M SAM  
That’s real. “1% of $10B” is not.
---
## Sizing Mistakes (3 of them)
### Mistake #1: Wrong market definition
Your TAM is not your industry.
It’s your specific buyer pool.
Examples:
- Selling medical software → “$1.1T US healthcare spending”
  - Correct: medical software buyers in your segment × ACV
- Selling pet health tech → “$320B global pet industry”
  - Correct: vet clinics + pet owners who pay × pricing
- Selling to restaurants → “$800B global food service market”
  - Correct: restaurants using digital tools × subscription fee
Rule of thumb:
If you removed your startup’s name and put a competitor’s, the TAM should stay the same.
### Mistake #2: No assumptions behind the numbers
Three big numbers with zero methodology doesn’t prove anything.
Instead include:
- customer count (e.g., “X companies in Moldova matching our ICP”)
- pricing assumption (e.g., “ACV is €Y based on competitor pricing + interviews”)
- penetration logic (e.g., “year 1 we target Z% based on execution_flow”)
### Mistake #3: Sizing the wrong thing entirely
Your TAM does not equal the market you’ll actually compete in.
Common failure patterns:
- founders Google the industry’s largest number
- then never connect it to the real business
Fix:
- build TAM around the *actual buyer pool*
- size the first launch geography/segment first
- don’t blur B2B vs B2C or “both” vs “one true beachhead”

## The Beachhead Principle (start small, win completely, then expand)
“Own a small market first. Dominate it. Then expand. This is the only path.”
- **Beachhead:** 10–100 customers you can name
- **Niche:** same pain, new segments
- **Vertical:** new markets, same ICP
- **Scale:** geographic expansion
Examples:
- Salvia: 3 cafes in Chisinau → not “all of Moldova food waste market”
- Friendly: students at one city → not “all urban singles globally”
- VineGuard: 20 vineyards in one region → prove ROI before scaling EU-wide

## 05 AI as Your Research Tool (accelerator, not replacement)
### Why AI is legitimate here
Claude doesn’t replace talking to customers.
Human conversations remain the highest-signal research you can do.
### What AI will do for you
It removes 80% of the grunt work before those conversations.
Use AI for:
- market landscape mapping
- ICP hypothesis building
- bottom-up size calculation
- interview question design
- competitive intelligence synthesis

## AI Prompts (copy/paste)
### Prompt #01 — Market Overview
```text
I'm building [describe your startup in 1–2 sentences].
Help me understand the market landscape:
What are the main market segments I could be playing in?
Who are the key players in each segment (established + emerging)?
What are the most common customer pain points in this space?
What's the rough market size range I should be looking at?
What trends are driving or slowing this market right now?
Be specific. If you're unsure about a number, say so.
Add your geography, stage, and business model for tighter outputs.

Prompt #02 — Define your Ideal Customer Profile
I'm building [product]. It solves [problem] for [who you think the user is].
Generate 2–3 ICP profiles. For each include:
Job title / role / company type
The specific pain they have (before they find me)
What they currently do to solve it (and why it's not enough)
What would trigger them to buy something new
Where I can find and reach them (channels, communities)
Rate each ICP:
- ease of reach (1–5)
- pain intensity (1–5)
Prompt #03 — Build a Bottom-Up Market Size
Help me build a bottom-up market size for my startup.
My product: [describe it]
My ICP: [buyer — job title, company size, geography]
My pricing model: [e.g. €X/month per user, or €Y/year per company]
My planned launch market: [e.g. Moldova + Romania, B2B SaaS]
Build:
1. TAM: global potential if I served everyone
2. SAM: segment I can realistically reach in 2–3 years
3. SOM: what I can win in year 1–2 given my stage + GTM
Show the math. Flag any weak assumptions.
Share the output with a mentor/investor: “Does this feel right?” Their reaction tells you everything.

Prompt #04 — Design Customer Interviews
I'm about to interview potential customers for my startup.
My startup: [1 sentence]
Who I'm interviewing: [title, company type]
What I want to learn: [current process, what's broken, willingness to pay]
Generate 15 discovery interview questions. Rules:
No leading questions (don't mention my product)
Focus on behavior, not opinions ("What did you do" > "What would you do")
Include: current process, cost, frequency, frustration
End with 2 questions that reveal willingness to pay
After 5 interviews: Paste notes and ask Claude to find patterns.

Prompt #05 — Analyze Competition
I'm building [startup]. My top 3–5 competitors: [List names + 1-line description each]
For each competitor:
Who their ICP appears to be (messaging, pricing, case studies)
Their main value prop and positioning
What they do well (honest)
Gaps or complaints in reviews / online feedback
What their pricing signals about their target segment
Then:
Where is the clearest positioning gap I can own?
Paste 10–15 G2/Capterra reviews per competitor. Ask: “What do customers wish this did differently?”

Prompt #06 — Sanity Check Your Market Slide
Here is the market sizing from my pitch deck:
TAM: [number + how you got it]
SAM: [number + how you got it]
SOM: [number + how you got it]
My startup: [describe it]
My ICP: [describe the buyer]
My launch market: [geography + segment]
Act as a skeptical early-stage investor. Tell me:
Which assumptions are weakest and why
Whether my TAM is too broad or too narrow
Whether my SOM is realistic for my stage + GTM
What questions you'd ask me in a pitch meeting
What I must fix before presenting this
06 The Honest Market Test (5 questions before you size anything)
If you can’t answer these, your market size doesn’t matter yet.

Can you name 20 specific potential customers today? If no: your SAM isn’t real yet.
What do these customers currently pay to solve this problem? No current spend usually means no validated willingness to pay.
Who already exists in this space, and how big are they? Zero competition usually means no market, not “blue ocean.”
Why would they switch to you specifically (in one sentence)? If you can’t answer: positioning isn’t ready.
What’s your beachhead — the smallest segment you can dominate? If the answer is “everyone”: you have no GTM. Start over.
Key Takeaways
TAM is ego. SOM is strategy.
Know which one you’re actually building toward.
Bottom-up beats top-down. Always.
Show your math. Every assumption defensible in 30 seconds.
Talk to 10 customers before trusting any report.
Primary research is the only research you can’t skip.
Start with a beachhead. Win it completely.
Then expand. Everywhere at once = nowhere.
Use AI to accelerate, not to replace thinking.
Research isn’t a milestone. It’s a habit.
The best founders constantly refine their market understanding.
